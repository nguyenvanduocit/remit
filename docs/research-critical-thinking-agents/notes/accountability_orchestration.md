## 1. Osmani's claims: what "orchestration tax" and "a human has to take responsibility" actually say

### Takeaway
Osmani sets the safe level of agent parallelism by one human's review rate, not by tool capacity. Agents can be asked to choose and route inside a policy, but only people "inherit consequence". The claim is a normative argument with no data behind it. The orchestration-tax post cites no statistics.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: "The orchestration tax is the structural gap between agent production and what you can actually merge." HIGH. [Osmani, The Orchestration Tax, 2026-05-24](https://addyosmani.com/blog/orchestration-tax/)
- Sizing rule: "The right number of parallel agents is how many you can actually code review properly." The article uses Amdahl's Law as the analogy: serial human review caps the speedup from parallel agents. HIGH. [Orchestration Tax](https://addyosmani.com/blog/orchestration-tax/)
- Recommended patterns in that post: backpressure (size the fleet to review rate); sorting work (isolated tasks to background agents, judgement-heavy work such as architecture and odd bugs kept serial and focused); batching reviews to cut context-switch cost; making agents prove routine work themselves ("Don't waste your brain on things the machine can verify itself"); protecting serial deep-work time. It cites Margaret-Anne Storey on cognitive debt. It has no risk tiers and no numbers (agent counts are illustrative). HIGH. [Orchestration Tax](https://addyosmani.com/blog/orchestration-tax/)
- "a human must take responsibility" for AI-contributed work. The post quotes the 1979 IBM training line "A computer can never be held accountable", and sets the rule "if you haven't seen the code do the right thing yourself, it doesn't work" and "Never commit code you can't explain." HIGH (via fetch summary). [Osmani, Code Review in the Age of AI, 2026-01-05](https://addyo.substack.com/p/code-review-in-the-age-of-ai)
- The same post proposes risk tiers: code touching auth, payments, secrets or untrusted input gets a human threat-model review plus a security tool pass. It also proposes a "PR contract": intent, proof it works, risk tier, which parts AI wrote, and the 1–2 areas that need a human look. Policy: "No PR goes up without either new tests or a demo of the change working." AI should "accelerate the process, never abdicate it." HIGH (via fetch summary). [Code Review in the Age of AI](https://addyo.substack.com/p/code-review-in-the-age-of-ai)
- Third-party numbers that post relays, not checked here: Jellyfish, PRs about 18% larger; Cortex 2026, incidents per PR up about 24% and change failure rate up about 30%; CodeRabbit, logic errors 1.75x the human rate and XSS 2.74x; Veracode, about 45% of AI code has security flaws; Graphite, tuned AI reviewers catch 70–80% of "low-hanging fruit". MEDIUM (secondary, relayed by Osmani). [Code Review in the Age of AI](https://addyo.substack.com/p/code-review-in-the-age-of-ai)
- "Own the Outer Loop": "Only people can choose. Only people inherit consequence." Agents "can be asked to choose, route, merge, and escalate safely inside a policy, but they cannot inherit the consequences." "If nobody owns the consequence of a decision, then high agency can only bring chaos." Work is done when "an independent check, not the model's own say-so, decides." Humans sit in the constraints, sampling, audit and ownership loops, not in the inner loop. The post proposes a per-codebase "accountability contract": the checklist, the evidence, who was accountable, and the status after a block. Verdict options: ship, block, redirect, narrow, add a guardrail, reject. HIGH (via fetch summary). [Osmani, Own the Outer Loop, 2026-07-15](https://addyosmani.com/blog/own-the-outer-loop/)
- Dates for "Own the Outer Loop" differ across aggregators (July 8, July 9, Sept 9, 2026). The primary page gives July 15, 2026. MEDIUM. [Latent Space wiki](https://latent.space/wiki/addy-osmani/); [primary](https://addyosmani.com/blog/own-the-outer-loop/)

### Inferences
- Osmani's two remedies pull against each other. "See it work yourself / never commit code you can't explain" needs line-level understanding. "Size the fleet to review rate / let machines verify" accepts that the human will not read everything. These are compatible only if "see it work" means evidence of behaviour (tests, demos, canaries), not reading the diff. MEDIUM (inference).
- "Accountability" in these posts is about who answers for the outcome. It is not a claim that the person had effective control. That gap is exactly what Elish's moral-crumple-zone critique targets (section 2). MEDIUM (inference).

## 2. Meaningful human control, the moral crumple zone, and human-in-the-loop as liability sponge

### Takeaway
The research says that putting a human approver at the end of an automated pipeline often moves blame without giving that person control. A developer who rubber-stamps agent PRs they could not realistically understand is a textbook moral crumple zone. The fix is to change the institution (evidence, tiering, a stop button, owning the system design), not to tell the human to review harder.

### Cited Findings
- Elish defines the "moral crumple zone" as responsibility "misattributed to a human actor who had limited control over the behavior of an automated or autonomous system". The human becomes the component that absorbs moral and legal blame, protecting "the integrity of the technological system, at the expense of the nearest human operator". Cases: Three Mile Island (1979), Air France 447 (2009), the Uber self-driving fatality. Citation: Elish, M.C. (2019), "Moral Crumple Zones: Cautionary Tales in Human-Robot Interaction", *Engaging Science, Technology, and Society* 5:40–60, DOI 10.17351/ests2019.260. HIGH (abstract read). [ESTS journal](https://estsjournal.org/index.php/ests/article/view/260); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/97ff6743ea7a44a5ade2a04fd2c57a3c)
- Elish explains the mismatch: agency is distributed and control is "mediated through time and space", so the operator is held to account for outcomes they could not fully monitor or direct. HIGH (abstract). [ESTS](https://estsjournal.org/index.php/ests/article/view/260)
- Green (2022) surveyed 41 policies that mandate human oversight of government algorithms and found two flaws. (1) "evidence suggests that people are unable to perform the desired oversight functions". (2) Oversight requirements "legitimize government use of flawed and controversial algorithms without addressing the fundamental issues". They give "a false sense of security" and "enable vendors and agencies to shirk accountability". Green's remedy is to move from human oversight to institutional oversight: justify that the algorithm belongs in the process and that the proposed oversight works, with evidence, before adoption. HIGH (abstract). [Green, Computer Law & Security Review 2022, arXiv 2109.05067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05067)
- Lee & See (2004) argue that trust guides reliance when full understanding is impractical. Trust should be calibrated (matched to real trustworthiness), have high resolution (change as capability changes) and be appropriately specific (tied to a component and a time). Citation: *Human Factors* 46(1):50–80, DOI 10.1518/hfes.46.1.50.30392. MEDIUM (abstract plus secondary summaries of the three constructs). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15151155/); [arXiv 2204.13828 summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.13828)
- Microsoft (Passi & Vorvoreanu, MSR-TR-2022-12, June 2022, review of about 60 papers) defines overreliance as "users start accepting incorrect AI outputs". The design goal is appropriate reliance: accept correct outputs, reject incorrect ones. Follow-ups: MSR-TR-2024-7 on GenAI, and MSR-TR-2025-4, which notes that many mitigations "backfire, actually increasing overreliance". MEDIUM (abstract and catalogue pages only). [MSR](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=854967); [Microsoft Learn playbook](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/ai/playbook/technology-guidance/overreliance-on-ai/overreliance-on-ai); [Fostering appropriate reliance](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1080732)

### Inferences
- Applied to agent PRs: if one developer approves N agent PRs a day, faster than they can understand them (section 4 data), the approval does not express control. It records who gets blamed when a SEV happens. That fits Elish's definition and Green's "false sense of security". MEDIUM (inference from the cited theory).
- Green's institutional-oversight remedy maps onto software as follows. The accountable human should own the system that produces and checks changes (checks, tiers, rollout gates, a working stop) and answer for it. They should not be the per-diff reader. This matches Osmani's "constraints, sampling, audit, ownership loops". MEDIUM (inference).
- Lee & See's "specificity" argues for trust per area, not one global "I trust the agent". For example, an owner can trust agent CSS changes and distrust agent auth changes, with separate track records. LOW–MEDIUM (inference).
- The Microsoft finding that some mitigations backfire is a warning for "comprehension quizzes" and forced-explanation UIs. They need empirical checks, not assumptions. MEDIUM (inference from cited abstract).

### Gaps
- The phrase "liability sponge" turned up no academic source. The closest established terms are Elish's "moral crumple zone" and Green's "shirk accountability". Dan Davies' "accountability sink" (*The Unaccountability Machine*, 2024) is related but was not checked this session.
- I found no empirical study of moral-crumple-zone dynamics in software code review specifically.

## 3. Regulation and standards: what the overseer is required to be able to do

### Takeaway
EU AI Act Article 14 is the only text found that names automation bias in law. It requires overseers to be *enabled* to understand, interpret, override and stop. NIST AI RMF and AI 600-1 are voluntary and ask for defined oversight roles plus management of "human-AI configuration" risk. Neither sets a volume ceiling. Article 14's capabilities are not achievable through line-by-line review at agent volume, but they can be met through system design (interpretation tools, a stop, override).

### Cited Findings
- Art. 14(1): high-risk AI systems must be designed so they "can be effectively overseen by natural persons during the period in which they are in use", including through suitable human-machine interface tools. HIGH. [EU AI Act Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- Art. 14(4), "as appropriate and proportionate", overseers must be enabled to: (a) understand capacities and limitations, monitor operation, detect anomalies and dysfunctions; (b) "remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output" (automation bias), especially where the system advises human decisions; (c) correctly interpret output; (d) decide not to use it, or to disregard, override or reverse its output; (e) intervene or halt via a "'stop' button or a similar procedure". HIGH. [Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- Art. 14(5): for remote biometric identification (Annex III 1(a)), no action may be taken unless the result is "separately verified and confirmed by at least two natural persons" with competence, training and authority. This is a four-eyes rule written into law for the highest-risk tier. HIGH. [Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- Timing: the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, published in the OJ 24 July 2026, in force 27 July 2026) moves the start of high-risk obligations, Art. 14 included, from 2 Aug 2026 to **2 Dec 2027** for stand-alone Annex III systems and **2 Aug 2028** for Annex I product-embedded systems. Art. 50 transparency duties stayed on 2 Aug 2026. HIGH: read in the regulation's own text. Publications Office metadata gives the title "… amending Regulations (EU) 2024/1689, (EU) 2018/1139 and (EU) 2023/1230 … (Digital Omnibus on AI)", signed 2026-07-08, published 2026-07-24, in force 2026-07-27. The amended Art. 113(c) reads: "Chapter III, Sections 1, 2, and 3, with the exception of Article 6(5), shall apply from: (i) 2 December 2027 as regards AI systems classified as high-risk pursuant to Article 6(2) and Annex III; and (ii) 2 August 2028 as regards AI systems classified as high-risk pursuant to Article 6(1) and Annex I". Art. 14 sits in Chapter III Section 2, so these dates govern it. [Publications Office, CELEX 32026R1744](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32026R1744) Some earlier sources (e.g. older Gibson Dunn alerts) still say publication is pending; they predate July 2026. [Cloud Security Alliance note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-eu-ai-act-high-risk-deadline-omnibus-20260/); [Mondaq, Omnibus enters into force](https://www.mondaq.com/eu-digital-omnibus-on-ai-enters-into-force/1825722); [SRD Rechtsanwälte](https://www.srd-rechtsanwaelte.de/en/blog/digital-omnibus-ai-what-is-changing-in-the-ai-act); [artificialintelligenceact.eu Art. 14/113](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- NIST AI RMF GOVERN 3.2 (AI 100-1), verbatim from the PDF: "Policies and procedures are in place to define and differentiate roles and responsibilities for human-AI configurations and oversight of AI systems." HIGH (primary). [NIST AI 100-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf)
- NIST AI 600-1 (GenAI Profile, July 2024), risk 7, verbatim: "Human-AI Configuration: Arrangements of or interactions between a human and an AI system which can result in the human inappropriately anthropomorphizing GAI systems or experiencing algorithmic aversion, automation bias, over-reliance, or emotional entanglement with GAI systems." HIGH (primary PDF text extracted with pdftotext). [NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)
- NIST AI 600-1 on automation bias, verbatim: "due to the complexity and increasing reliability of GAI technology, over time, humans may over-rely on GAI systems or may unjustifiably perceive GAI content to be of higher quality than that produced by other sources. This phenomenon is an example of automation bias, or excessive deference to automated systems." HIGH (primary). [NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)
- NIST AI 600-1 suggested actions under GOVERN 3.2 (primary text): GV-3.2-001 "bolster oversight of GAI systems with independent evaluations or assessments … where the type and robustness of evaluations are proportional to the identified risks"; GV-3.2-002 adjust organisational roles across the lifecycle (test and evaluation, red-teaming, incident response and containment); GV-3.2-003 acceptable use policies for human-AI configurations; GV-3.2-004 user feedback and recourse mechanisms; GV-3.2-005 threat modelling. These are "suggested actions", not requirements. HIGH (primary). [NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- A coding agent is usually not a "high-risk AI system" under Annex III. Article 14 binds a developer's agent fleet only when the software it writes is part of a regulated high-risk system. It is still the clearest checklist for what *effective* oversight means. MEDIUM (inference; the scope reading has not been checked by a lawyer).
- Article 14 phrases each duty as "enabled to". The obligation falls on the system's design (provider), not on the human's stamina. That matches Green's institutional-oversight view and undercuts "the developer approved it, so the developer is responsible" as a sufficient design. MEDIUM (inference).
- At agent volume: (a), (c), (d) and (e) are achievable through tooling (dashboards, explanations, revert, pause/stop). (b), awareness of automation bias, is the hardest, because review fatigue at high volume is the very condition that produces automation bias. LOW–MEDIUM (inference).

- Both NIST PDFs (AI 600-1 and AI 100-1) were read in full text.

## 4. Review capacity math: agent output vs human review bandwidth (2025–2026 evidence)

### Takeaway
Independent sources agree on the direction. AI raises output (PRs, PR size). Review time and instability rise with it, and organisation-level delivery gains stay small. The bottleneck has moved to review and verification. The numbers come mostly from vendor telemetry and surveys, and they differ in size.

### Cited Findings
- DORA 2025, "State of AI-assisted Software Development": about 5,000 respondents plus 100+ hours of qualitative data. 90% use AI at work, over 80% believe it raised their productivity, and 30% report little or no trust in AI-generated code. AI adoption now has a positive relationship with throughput and product performance (in 2024 the throughput link was negative) and "continues to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability". HIGH. Confirmed in the report PDF: "AI adoption now improves software delivery throughput … However, it still increases delivery instability. This suggests that while teams are adapting for speed, their underlying systems have not yet evolved to safely manage AI-accelerated development." The 30% low-trust figure indicates "a need for critical validation skills". [DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)
- DORA's own explanation of the instability, verbatim from the report PDF: "We have hypothesized that this is likely, in part, because it is harder to review larger batches of code", which stresses "the importance of being able to rapidly undo changes when working with larger batches of code". The foreword invokes the Nyquist stability criterion: a control system "must operate at least twice as fast as the system it controls". Applied here, review and feedback must outpace generation. Usage table: 71% of respondents who write code use AI to do it. HIGH (primary). [DORA 2025 report PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf) [Google Cloud blog, DORA 2025](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report)
- DORA's stated mechanism: higher change volume exposes weaknesses downstream. Without strong automated testing, mature version control and fast feedback loops, it produces instability. "AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there." User-centric focus and quality internal platforms amplify the gains, while tightly coupled systems see little benefit. DORA also published an AI Capabilities Model (seven capabilities) in Dec 2025. HIGH. [Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report); [DORA year in review](https://dora.dev/insights/dora-2025-year-in-review/)

[TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/searchSoftwareQuality/news/366631712/Google-DORA-Software-delivery-caught-up-to-AI-coding-tools); [RedMonk](https://redmonk.com/rstephens/2025/12/18/dora2025/)

MEDIUM (vendor telemetry, relayed by secondary sources; Faros sells measurement tooling). ## 5. Design patterns for calibrated oversight at scale

### Takeaway
The patterns with the strongest evidence share one move: spend scarce human attention according to risk, and let deterministic checks and staged rollout carry the rest. Meta's diff-risk-score gating is the best published evidence that risk-tiered, partly automated review works at scale. Comprehension quizzes and decision logs have design rationale but almost no published outcome data.

### Cited Findings
- **Risk-tiered gating (Meta, Diff Risk Score).** Meta trained a model on historical SEV-causing diffs to predict each diff's risk and gate diffs above a threshold. A logistic-regression baseline captured 18.7% / 27.9% / 84.6% of SEVs while gating the top 5% / 10% / 50% riskiest diffs. That is a large share of incidents for a small share of diffs, which is the core argument for risk tiers. HIGH (primary arXiv, ICSE 2025 SEIP). [arXiv 2410.06351](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.06351v1); [ICSE 2025](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2025/icse-2025-software-engineering-in-practice/18/Moving-Faster-and-Reducing-Risk-Using-LLMs-in-Release-Deployment)
- **Automated review for low-risk diffs (Meta, RADAR).** Adams et al. (30 Meta authors, including Mockus, Rigby and Nagappan), "Automating Low-Risk Code Review at Meta: RADAR, Risk Calibration, and Review Efficiency", arXiv:2605.30208v2, June 2026. A multi-stage funnel of eligibility checks, static heuristics, Diff Risk Score, LLM review and deterministic validation auto-reviews and lands low-to-medium complexity diffs, and sends riskier ones to humans. Results: 535,290 diffs reviewed, 331,720 landed, peak about 25K a day. Revert rate is 1/3 and production-incident rate 1/50 of non-RADAR diffs (Fisher's exact p<1e-16 and p<1e-6). Median review wall time is 35% lower. HIGH that the paper reports this (primary read). **Caveats the paper itself states:** RADAR assignment was not random, so confounding is possible, and low-risk diffs are routed to RADAR by design. Incident attribution is imperfect when changes land close together. The time figures are observational difference-in-differences estimates. The "over 330%" time-to-close reduction appears five times in the PDF text (abstract, Table 4 "over 330% reduction", discussion, conclusion). It is the paper's own wording, not an extraction error. The RQ3 text says "the median time to close is over 330% lower … than for human-reviewed diffs", and Table 6 says "over 330% reduction". Neither defines the base or gives the raw medians. A reduction above 100% is impossible, so it most likely means human-reviewed diffs take about 4.3x longer to close. Cite it as "time to close several times shorter", not as a percentage. The 35% review wall-time figure is well formed. Human backstops are a configurable landing delay during which a human can reject, authors able to return a diff to "Needs Review", and threshold-tuning feedback loops. The paper describes no random audit of RADAR-landed diffs. MEDIUM for the causal claim. [arXiv 2605.30208](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30208)
- **Risk tiers by domain plus a PR contract** (intent, proof, risk tier, AI-written parts, 1–2 areas for human focus): auth, payments, secrets and untrusted input always get human threat-model review. HIGH (that Osmani recommends it). [Code Review in the Age of AI](https://addyo.substack.com/p/code-review-in-the-age-of-ai)
- **Evidence-first completion:** "No PR goes up without either new tests or a demo". Agents prove routine work with tests or screenshots. "Done" is decided by an independent check, not the model's own word. HIGH (Osmani's recommendation). [Code Review…](https://addyo.substack.com/p/code-review-in-the-age-of-ai); [Orchestration Tax](https://addyosmani.com/blog/orchestration-tax/); [Own the Outer Loop](https://addyosmani.com/blog/own-the-outer-loop/)
- **Sampling and audit loops; an accountability contract per codebase** (checklist, evidence, who was accountable, status after block); verdict options beyond approve/reject (narrow, redirect, add a guardrail). HIGH (recommendation). [Own the Outer Loop](https://addyosmani.com/blog/own-the-outer-loop/)
- **Backpressure, batching, sorting work by judgement load.** HIGH (recommendation). [Orchestration Tax](https://addyosmani.com/blog/orchestration-tax/)
- **Strong safety nets as an enabler:** DORA lists "fortify safety nets", "prioritize foundational practices" (automated testing, version control, fast feedback) and small, loosely coupled changes as the conditions under which AI throughput does not become instability. HIGH. [DORA 2025 announcement](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report)
- **Stop button and override as a required capability:** EU AI Act Art. 14(4)(d)–(e). HIGH. [Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- **Two-person verification for the highest-risk decisions:** Art. 14(5). HIGH. [Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- **Caution on reliance interventions:** Microsoft reports that many overreliance mitigations backfire and *increase* overreliance. HIGH that the claim is made; the details are unread. [MSR fostering appropriate reliance](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1080732)
- **Sampling in high-reliability settings (closest evidence found).** Parasuraman et al. (1993): operators detected automation failures substantially worse when automation reliability stayed *constant* than when it varied (complacency, after about 20 minutes). Aviation quality-audit practice uses risk-weighted random, stratified or cluster sampling, and warns that a clean sampled audit is not proof of operational excellence. HRO practice accepts extra overhead in auditing and testing as the price of reliability. MEDIUM (NASA record for Parasuraman; trade and professional sources for audit practice; IFATCA working paper for HRO). [NASA NTRS, Parasuraman 1993](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930055574); [Sofema, sampling in aviation quality audits](https://sofemaonline.com/about/blog/entry/how-to-deliver-effective-sampling-during-aviation-quality-auditing-2); [IFATCA WP 2025-152](https://ifatca.org/wp-content/uploads/WP-2025-152.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Canary or staged rollout as a substitute for line-by-line review.** Meta's data shows risk concentrates in a minority of diffs. For the low-risk majority, an observed production signal (canary metrics, automatic rollback) checks behaviour more cheaply and more directly than reading the diff. This works only if rollback is cheap and the blast radius is bounded. MEDIUM (inference from Meta DRS plus DORA safety-net findings).
- **Trust-but-verify sampling.** Auditing a random X% of fully delegated changes produces an unbiased estimate of the escape-defect rate per tier. That gives Lee & See's "calibration" and "resolution" a measurable basis: tier thresholds move when the sampled defect rate moves. MEDIUM (inference; no software-specific study found).
- **Agents triaging attention.** RADAR is an existence proof that a pipeline can decide "this needs a human". The router itself then becomes a high-stakes, accountable component, and its misses must be audited. MEDIUM (inference).
- **Decision and assumption logs.** These give the accountable human what Art. 14(4)(c) calls the ability to "correctly interpret" output, after the fact. No outcome data found. LOW–MEDIUM.
- **Comprehension quizzes before merge.** These target Osmani's "never commit code you can't explain". Given Microsoft's backfire finding, they should be piloted with measured effect (escape defects, time). LOW.

### Gaps
- I found no published controlled evaluation of comprehension quizzes, assumption logs or evidence-first completion reports on review quality.
- The HRO literature was searched. I found no study that tests whether sampling audits catch automation-induced failures. Parasuraman's result implies that a reliable agent fleet makes human complacency worse, which argues for injected or known-bad samples to keep reviewers alert (inference, LOW–MEDIUM).
- The RADAR primary paper was read. It does not describe random human audit of auto-landed diffs, so no published at-scale sampling-audit result for agent code exists in the sources found.

## 6. Where is the line: decisions that should never be fully delegated

### Takeaway
Sources converge on three things humans keep: choosing what is worth doing, answering for consequences (including safety to ship), and high-risk domains (security, money, secrets, untrusted input). Execution, routine verification and routing within a policy can be delegated. The line is drawn by who bears the consequence, not by task difficulty.

### Cited Findings
- Humans keep "choosing what is worth doing"; the bottleneck becomes "should this exist, can we answer for it?". Someone "must explain why it should exist, why it's safe enough to be part of production". Humans decide "if the evidence is sufficient to proceed". Agents may "choose, route, merge, and escalate safely inside a policy". HIGH (via fetch summary). [Own the Outer Loop](https://addyosmani.com/blog/own-the-outer-loop/)
- Never delegate accountability, security review of high-risk code, intent and roadmap context, or verification itself ("never abdicate it"). Never commit code you can't explain. HIGH (via fetch summary). [Code Review in the Age of AI](https://addyo.substack.com/p/code-review-in-the-age-of-ai)
- Judgement-heavy work (architecture, odd bugs) is kept for focused human attention and not parallelised. HIGH. [Orchestration Tax](https://addyosmani.com/blog/orchestration-tax/)
- Legal floor for the highest-risk class: two competent humans must confirm before action (Art. 14(5)), and overseers must be able to override or stop (14(4)(d)–(e)). HIGH. [EU AI Act Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- Institutional floor: the decision *to adopt* an automated system, and the evidence that its oversight works, should be justified and reviewed before deployment (Green). HIGH (abstract). [Green 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05067)
- "Who it impacts" is not addressed directly in "Own the Outer Loop". The closest wording is "caring for the result". HIGH (absence noted by fetch). [Own the Outer Loop](https://addyosmani.com/blog/own-the-outer-loop/)

### Inferences
- A workable rule from the sources: delegate fully where being wrong is cheap, detectable and reversible (canary-able, revertible, small blast radius). Keep a human decision where a wrong outcome is irreversible, hard to detect, or falls on people outside the team (customers, security, money, legal). This combines Meta's risk concentration, DORA's safety nets, Art. 14(5) for irreversible high-stakes cases, and Osmani's "inherit consequence". MEDIUM (synthesis).
- To avoid the moral crumple zone, the human's non-delegable role should be defined as ownership of the *policy* (tiers, thresholds, checks, stop) plus the *outcome*, with the tooling Art. 14 lists. It should not be defined as a signature on every diff. A signature without that tooling is liability without control. MEDIUM (synthesis of Elish, Green and Art. 14).

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found that tests where the delegation line should sit for coding agents specifically.
